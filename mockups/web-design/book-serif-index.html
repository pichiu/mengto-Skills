VOL. II · CH. 03 A HOUSE DIVIDED PP. 44–45

Chapter Three

A House
Divided

In which the estate is measured, and found wanting, by those who claim it.

The letter arrived on the ninth of October, sealed with a wax that had already begun to crack along its edge, as though the message inside were too heavy for its casing to bear. It named no sender, only an address in a hand that Eleanor recognized from earlier correspondence — hurried, slanted, pressed hard enough to leave a shadow on the page beneath.

She read it twice before setting it down. The first reading was for the words. The second was for what sat beneath them — the accusations folded so carefully into courtesy that a careless reader might miss them entirely, mistaking grievance for concern, and concern for love.

Marginal note — ed.

The original manuscript bears a faint pencil annotation here, likely added decades later: "she knew before she read it twice."

— 44 —

By the time the second letter came, the household had already begun to reorganize itself around the absence it implied. Servants spoke less. The study door, once left open through the afternoon, was now kept closed until supper. Even the light seemed to arrive differently through the east windows, thinner, as if it too had been asked to keep its distance.

“A house does not divide in a single evening. It divides in the small refusals that come before anyone has the courage to name them.”

— from the estate diaries, undated leaf

What follows in this chapter is drawn from three sources: the surviving correspondence, the household ledgers cross-referenced in Part Two, and a set of diary fragments recovered from the east wing after the fire. Where these accounts disagree, the discrepancy is noted rather than resolved.
